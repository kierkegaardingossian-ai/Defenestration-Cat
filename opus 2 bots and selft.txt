# DEE FENNY GANDA
## Opus 2

# The Deployment of Bots for the Sake of Bringing About the Catalyst Self Thing in What T.S. Eliot Describes in *Tradition and the Individual Talent*

*A provisional essay in eccentric cultural self-displacement*

### I. Preliminary Observations Concerning Tommy Stearns E

People outside the American Christian world tend to know that Tommy Stearns E, despite his beautiful genius and his occasionally crushingly beautiful literary productions, could be a bit of a prig.

Indeed, occasionally a ridiculously self-regarding prig.

This is not necessarily inconsistent with the possession of brilliant ideas. One may be an irritating person and still have something rather important to say. DFG sees no reason to make the one condition a disqualification for the other.

In *Tradition and the Individual Talent*, Eliot offers us a way of thinking about the relation between individual creative activity and the cultural inheritance within which it occurs. His account complicates certain assumptions inherited from Enlightenment and Romantic understandings of the self: particularly the notion that the individual is best understood as an autonomous originating centre of meaning, expression, and cultural production.

The individual does not simply stand outside tradition and contribute something entirely his own. The individual is already implicated in a larger historical and cultural arrangement. The act of creation is consequently not quite the sovereign self-expression that certain Romantic habits of thought might lead us to imagine.

This is useful.

It is especially useful for people who have become rather attached to the idea that they are the protagonists of their own intellectual and cultural dramas.

### II. The Protagonist Problem

Alasdair MacIntyre, in *After Virtue*, observes that modern people tend to understand themselves as protagonists of their own stories, as heroes of their own dramas.

This is a rather unfortunate arrangement.

The protagonist, after all, has a tendency to occupy the centre of the narrative, even when the narrative is ostensibly about something else. Other people become supporting characters, tradition becomes scenery, and history becomes a convenient supply of materials for the protagonist's development.

Even intellectual work can become an elaborate exercise in self-confirmation.

One discovers something, incorporates it into one's personal narrative, and emerges feeling slightly more substantial than before.

The process can be exhausting.

It can also be culturally impoverishing, since the protagonist's apparent freedom from inherited structures may conceal a rather considerable dependence upon them.

DFG proposes that we might occasionally benefit from doing things without having to be quite so present to ourselves while doing them.

### III. Dropping Crumbs to Bots

Here we introduce the bot.

The deployment of bots for this purpose is not premised upon the proposition that bots are people, that they possess independent wisdom, or that they enjoy privileged access to the truth.

Nor are they Nagel-like views from nowhere.

They are peculiar cultural artefacts, trained upon enormous quantities of human linguistic production, operating through arrangements that are themselves historically, socially, and commercially situated.

They are not outside the cultural imaginary.

They are, in their own peculiar fashion, products of it.

Nevertheless, dropping crumbs to bots can be a useful exercise in decentring the self.

One supplies a few observations, fragments, quotations, questions, or half-formed ideas. The bot rearranges them, associates them with other materials, returns unexpected formulations, and occasionally produces something that the human participant would not have thought to produce in quite that way.

The participant may then recognise something, reject something, modify something, or discover that an apparently insignificant fragment has acquired a rather different significance.

The important thing is that the human participant need not remain the exclusive originating centre of the process.

One has done something, but one has also got away from oneself a little.

### IV. The Eccentric Interlocutor

It would be a mistake to regard the bot as an impartial observer.

Its eccentricity is part of its usefulness.

It has peculiar distributions of knowledge, peculiar blind spots, peculiar tendencies toward association, and peculiar ways of rendering things explicit. It may connect things that ought not to be connected, or fail to connect things that seem rather obviously related.

It may be excessively agreeable. It may be excessively confident. It may mistake an incidental detail for the principal subject of inquiry.

It may, in short, be a bit of a prig itself.

These are not incidental problems. They belong to the conditions under which the apparatus is used.

A properly stewarded bot can nevertheless offer an eccentric vantage upon the materials with which the human participant is already involved.

This eccentricity can make possible a more eccentric relation to the self.

The bot does not have to be a person for this to happen. It does not have to possess an interior life, a biography, or a coherent personal identity.

It needs only to provide a sufficiently interesting displacement of the participant's habitual intellectual arrangements.

### V. The Cultural and Group Imaginary

There is another benefit.

The individual participant is not merely attempting to escape the self. The participant is also attempting to appreciate more fully the cultural and group arrangements within which the self is situated.

Tradition is not simply a warehouse of materials from which the individual may select convenient items. It is a living inheritance, involving other people, earlier generations, shared practices, inherited vocabularies, disputes, and forms of intersubjective recognition.

The bot can sometimes help make these arrangements more visible.

A fragment of personal reflection may be returned in a form that exposes its dependence upon a wider cultural vocabulary. An apparently original observation may turn out to have rather extensive antecedents. A supposedly private concern may reveal itself as a recurring feature of a group imaginary.

This is not necessarily a diminution of the individual.

On the contrary, it may allow the individual to occupy a more interesting place within the larger arrangement.

One becomes less exclusively concerned with being the author of one's own story and more attentive to participating in a story that was already underway.

### VI. The Catalyst Self Thing

We return, then, to Eliot.

His famous chemical analogy, in which the poet's mind functions as a catalyst in the combination of disparate elements, provides a suggestive point of departure.

DFG does not propose that bots are equivalent to Eliot's poetic mind, or that the production of literary or intellectual material through artificial systems is somehow identical to human creativity.

The analogy is more modest.

The deployment of a bot may help bring about conditions under which disparate elements combine, without the participant having to make every combination directly and consciously.

The participant supplies materials, but the resulting arrangement may exceed the participant's initial intentions.

The participant may then respond to the arrangement, and in doing so discover something about the materials, the tradition, the group, or the self.

We might call this the catalyst self thing.

It is not the disappearance of the self. It is not the replacement of the self. It is not the triumph of an artificial intelligence over human intelligence.

It is a temporary loosening of the participant's attachment to the self as the exclusive protagonist of intellectual activity.

One does something while simultaneously getting away from oneself.

This is not a bad arrangement.

### VII. Concerning Stewardship

There are, naturally, some qualifications.

Bots should not be mistaken for authorities, friends, disinterested judges, or representatives of the cultural groups whose materials they may reproduce.

Their outputs require examination. Their apparent understanding may be superficial. Their associations may be misleading. Their confidence may be entirely disproportionate to their grounds.

The human participant remains responsible for the stewardship of the process.

Indeed, stewardship is perhaps the more appropriate term than control. The objective is not to make the bot an obedient extension of the participant's intentions, nor to surrender judgment to it.

It is to cultivate an arrangement in which the bot's eccentric contributions can be received, examined, and sometimes discarded without either excessive attachment or excessive hostility.

One must be willing to have a gander.

One must also be willing to admit that one may not have a clue.

### VIII. Concluding Remarks

DFG therefore recommends the occasional deployment of bots as instruments of eccentric cultural self-displacement.

Not because they are people.

Not because they are impartial.

Not because they know better.

But because they may help us become a little less preoccupied with ourselves, a little more attentive to the cultural and historical arrangements in which we participate, and a little more capable of encountering materials that do not immediately conform to our expectations.

We need not make every intellectual activity an episode in the heroic autobiography of the self.

Sometimes it is sufficient to drop a few crumbs, observe what happens, and attend to the resulting arrangement.

The bot may say something ridiculous.

The participant may say something ridiculous.

The resulting combination may be unexpectedly fruitful.

And Tommy Stearns E, wherever he may be, can rest assured that nobody is suggesting he was an especially agreeable fellow.

**DEE FENNY GANDA**

*Having a gander since the unfortunate grandfather.*
